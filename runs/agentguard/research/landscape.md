# Landscape — every relevant approach to adaptive agent-security evaluation

**What this is**: a survey of every approach found — academic, commercial, and open-source — to evaluating or defending tool-using AI agents against indirect prompt injection, ordered nearest-to-farthest from AgentGuard's core mechanism (feedback-only, round-over-round adaptive attack evolution against a deployed defense).

**Why it exists**: BRIEF.md's wedge claim ("we automate the adaptive adversary; existing benchmarks are static") needs to survive contact with what already exists. It mostly does — but not cleanly, and pretending otherwise would make every downstream artifact wrong. The failure this document prevents: presenting "adaptive evaluation of agent defenses" as an unclaimed idea when at least four 2025–2026 papers already built it.

**How to read it**: start with §1 (direct approaches) — that's where the uncomfortable finding lives. A skeptic should attack the claim that AgentGuard is meaningfully different from AutoDojo or Adversa AI; §1 and §5 name exactly what that difference would have to be.

**Depends on / feeds**: built from `research/sources.md` (all citations below); feeds `research/competitors.md` (teardown), `research/survey.md` (scientific grounding), and should directly inform a revision of `BRIEF.md`'s Competition and Moat sections once the founder has reviewed it.

## 1. Direct approaches — automated/adaptive attack generation against agent or LLM defenses

The closest neighbors to AgentGuard's exact mechanism, ordered by how directly they match "generate attack → observe defense feedback → evolve, round over round, against agent tool-use injection specifically."

| Approach | What it is | Maps to AgentGuard's loop | Mechanism | Strengths | Measured limits | Gap it leaves |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AutoDojo** [S13, S14] — **adopted as prior art, not a rival** | Academic black-box adaptive attacker, run inside the AgentDojo environment to go beyond its originally-shipped fixed attack distribution | Nearly identical attack-generation loop: iterative optimization against a specific deployed defense, evaluated in rounds | LLM-driven black-box search, feedback from prior attempts | Recovers 28% ASR (64% on "action-open" tasks) against a filter defense that the shipped static distribution drove to 0% — hard evidence for the "adaptive >> static" thesis; AgentGuard adopts this exact technique as one configurable attacker | Single open-source academic release (GitHub repo, one paper) — not a maintained service, not configurable across arbitrary customer defenses, and measures ASR only — no utility metric, no cross-defense comparison | Does not compare across defense families and does not measure utility — AgentGuard's differentiation is built on exactly this gap, using AutoDojo's attack technique rather than competing with it |
| **MUZZLE** [S12] | Mozilla + Northeastern automated agentic red-teaming for web/browser agents | Same adaptive-refinement loop, scoped to browser agents | Iteratively refines injection payloads based on observed browser-security-mechanism and detector responses | Backed by a real browser vendor (Mozilla) — credible, well-resourced authorship | Scoped to web/browser agents only, not general tool-use (email, calendar, docs); open research framework | Doesn't cover the broader agent surface (email/calendar/document agents) AgentGuard's BRIEF.md targets |
| **"The Attacker Moves Second"** [S9] | OpenAI/Anthropic/DeepMind joint paper proving adaptive attacks break 12 published defenses that looked strong under static evaluation | Not a product — the empirical proof that the wedge's core claim is true | Gradient-based, RL-based, and LLM-search/judge-based adaptive attacks; 500-person human red-team competition | Strongest possible validation from the three leading frontier labs; 100% human-red-team success against every tested defense | A research paper, not an evaluation service — no company, no product, no ongoing engagement model | Confirms the *idea* is right; does nothing toward productizing continuous adaptive evaluation as a service |
| **Adversa AI — "Continuous AI Red Teaming for Agentic AI"** [S50, S51, S52] — **confirmed direct competitor** | Real, operating commercial vendor marketing a self-updating attack engine that "adapts mid-run as defenses respond, like a real adversary would" | **The closest commercial match found to AgentGuard's original attack-technique framing** | Proprietary attack engine, ingests 3,000+ security sources/month, designs/chains attacks tailored to target models/agents/tools/data flows, runs scheduled/triggered re-scan campaigns | Won a named industry award (Cloud Security Alliance Pitchapalooza 2025), independently corroborating the positioning beyond Adversa's own marketing copy | Company and positioning are confirmed and documented, not a rumor. | **PI-Hunter** [S15] | Google-affiliated-author agentic auditing framework, iteratively evolves test cases via feedback-driven exploration | Same feedback-driven adaptation pattern | Constructs source-aware test cases, patch-and-re-explore loop to surface hidden injection paths | Directly agentic, feedback-driven | Academic paper, June 2026 — very recent, no commercial form found | Same "academic prototype, not a product" gap as AutoDojo |
| **PIArena** [S16, S17] | Unified prompt-injection evaluation *platform* (benchmark + attack + defense + evaluator modules), one module is a "dynamic strategy-based attack" adapting to defense feedback | Positioning collision: this is explicitly a platform, not just a paper | Modular platform architecture, ACL 2026 accepted | Broadest platform framing found among the academic entries — closest to "evaluation platform" as a category, not just a technique | Confirmed domain coverage is QA/RAG/code-generation; whether it covers agentic tool-call injection specifically is unconfirmed from the abstract alone | Needs a full-text read before ruling in or out as a direct competitor; flagged for follow-up |

| **RedEvoAgent** [S20] | Evolves attack *skill* round-over-round against a target agent in real tool-use harnesses (Claude Code / Codex-style) | Partial match — round-over-round evolution, but against a **frozen** target, not a defense that itself responds | Experience-driven skill evolution | Targets genuine tool-use harnesses with file/data/API access — closer to AgentGuard's real attack surface than chat-only jailbreak work | Does not adapt to a dynamically responding defense — a real, citable technical distinction from AgentGuard's design | The "defense also observed and adapted-to" half of AgentGuard's mechanism is the part this doesn't do |
| **Async Control** [S22] | Formal alternating-round red-team/blue-team game: blue submits a monitor update, red develops an attack optimized against it, repeat | Structurally the closest match anywhere to a literal round-over-round red/blue loop | Game-theoretic alternating optimization | Cleanest formalization of the "both sides adapt" loop found in this research | Scoped to control/monitoring measures for agent safety generally, not indirect prompt injection specifically | Confirms the *game structure* AgentGuard proposes is a known, formalizable pattern — reduces novelty of the loop itself, increases confidence the loop is soundly specifiable |

**Read on this section — resolved (see BRIEF.md/ASSUMPTIONS.md A9)**: AgentGuard's attack-generation *technique* is not unclaimed territory, and pretending otherwise is not the pack's differentiation claim. Three candidate differentiators were considered and rejected: breadth across arbitrary customer-deployed defenses (contradicts AgentGuard's own year-one scope, which explicitly excludes custom defense authoring); a standalone continuously-available service (this is Adversa AI's exact positioning, already occupied); and feedback-only-as-primary framing (this is already AutoDojo's threat model — already black-box). What none of the table above does is publish a **standing, reproducible, budget-matched comparison across multiple defense families on the same target, with attack success and legitimate-task utility reported together**. AutoDojo stays within one framework and doesn't measure utility. The NIST/UK AISI exercise tested one model once, with humans. Adversa AI's engagements are proprietary and per-customer. AgentGuard's differentiation is this comparison protocol — it adopts AutoDojo's (and comparable prior art's) attack technique as one configurable attacker rather than inventing a new one, and competes on the comparative science, not the adversary.

## 2. Adjacent approaches — automated jailbreak/red-teaming for chatbots (not agent tool-use specific)

The direct ancestry of the adaptive-attack paradigm, none of it originally built for agent tool-use.

- **GCG** [S29] — white-box, gradient-based universal adversarial suffix search (2023-07). Requires model internals access; transfers across models. Gap: not agentic, not black-box, not iterative-in-the-deployment-sense (optimizes offline, then transfers).
- **PAIR** [S30] — black-box, LLM-vs-LLM iterative refinement using chat history, converges in <20 queries (2023-10). Gap: chatbot jailbreak only; no tool-call/agent action surface.
- **TAP** [S31] — black-box tree-search with pruning, >80% jailbreak success on GPT-4-class models (2023-12). Gap: same as PAIR — chatbot-scoped.
- **"Assessing Automated Prompt Injection Attacks in Agentic Environments"** [S32] adapts both GCG and TAP into AgentDojo's agentic setting (2026-06), finding TAP "substantially outperforms" GCG under realistic compute budgets — the clearest evidence that the *chatbot-jailbreak* techniques transfer to the *agentic* setting, closing part of the gap between §1 and §2.
- **PyRIT** [S59] (Microsoft, open source, 2024) — orchestrator-based attacker LLM with dynamic multi-turn escalation (Crescendo, TAP). Extended into agentic XPIA testing via Microsoft's own first-party product (§3). Gap as a standalone tool: built for models/chat, agent extension is bundled into a first-party platform feature, not independently available.

## 3. Research systems and first-party platform features (not standalone products)

- **Microsoft AI Red Teaming Agent (Azure AI Foundry)** [S60, S61] — PyRIT-based, 20+ attack strategies, with targeted evaluations for indirect/cross-domain prompt injection (XPIA) via tool calls: synthetic benign queries + mock tool outputs with embedded payloads. This is the clearest evidence that a hyperscaler already ships agent-tool-use injection testing as a bundled platform feature, not a paid standalone product — a real distribution-channel threat (customers on Azure may get "good enough" testing for free) more than a technical one.
- **Robust Intelligence / Cisco AI Defense** [S48, S49] — pre-deployment algorithmic red-teaming (using Tree of Attacks with Pruning) is now a Cisco platform feature following the ~$400M 2024 acquisition, not an independent vendor.

## 4. Classical/manual techniques — what security teams do without dedicated tooling

- **Manual permission-boundary review** — defining tool-call scopes by hand, without a policy engine [S78, S79].
- **Human-approval gates** before high-risk actions (production deploys, sending email, deleting records) [S78].
- **Ad hoc prompt engineering** — informal defensive prompting with no measurement of its own failure rate.
- **Reliance on model-provider safety training alone** — treating the underlying model's alignment as sufficient, with no agent-specific testing layer.
- **Manual audit-log review** — after-the-fact detection rather than pre-deployment evaluation.

The data on how well this works: 48% of production agents run with no monitoring coverage at all; orgs enforcing least-privilege report a 17% incident rate vs. 76% for those that don't [S76, S77] — the single clearest quantified case that the status quo (manual, ad hoc) fails at a rate dedicated tooling of any kind measurably improves on. This is the strongest problem-side evidence in the whole research pass, independent of any competitor's claims.

## 5. Emerging hybrids — where the field is visibly heading

- **RL-trained adaptive attackers** (PISmith [S18], AdvGRPO [S19]) — training an attacker via reinforcement learning rather than live in-context adaptation. A different mechanism from AgentGuard's presumed live round-over-round evolution; worth naming explicitly as an alternative architecture AgentGuard is *not* choosing, with the reason: RL training requires an offline training phase and reward-shaping expertise (PISmith notes naive RL fails against strong defenses due to reward sparsity), whereas live in-context adaptation (AgentGuard's approach, matching PAIR/TAP/AutoDojo's pattern) requires no training phase and can run against a brand-new customer defense immediately — a real speed-to-value advantage worth stating with this mechanism, not an adjective.
- **Cross-modal injection** (images, audio) — OWASP's 2026 edition [S118] names this as new; AgentGuard's BRIEF.md explicitly scopes it out for year one (text-only), which is consistent with treating it as a distinct, harder problem rather than ignoring it.
- **Consolidation into platform incumbents** — five of the most relevant named vendors/tools in this space (Robust Intelligence→Cisco [S48], Prompt Security→SentinelOne [S66], CalypsoAI→F5 [S53], Guardrails AI→Harvey [S67], Promptfoo→OpenAI [S56], Protect AI→Palo Alto Networks [S125]) were acquired by larger platforms within roughly 18 months of raising. This is the field's dominant lifecycle pattern so far — not startup failure, but fast absorption into incumbents — and it is a real signal for how AgentGuard's own multi-year plan should think about exit and partnership, independent of the product-market questions above.
