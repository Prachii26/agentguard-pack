# Whitepaper — the comparison protocol, not the attacker

**What this is**: the mechanism-arithmetic case for AgentGuard as a real infrastructure contribution — a standing, budget-matched, utility-paired comparison protocol across defense families — not a claim to a novel attack technique.

**Why it exists**: `strategy/positioning.md` names the risk this document exists to defuse directly: "read uncharitably, AgentGuard's mechanism is an orchestration layer over two already-public academic components." A whitepaper that dodges that risk by leaning on attack-technique novelty would contradict `ASSUMPTIONS.md` A9 and `research/competitors.md`, and would not survive a second reading. The failure this document prevents: a pitch that claims AgentGuard "out-attacks" AutoDojo or Adversa AI — a claim two other artifacts in this pack have already disproven — instead of the narrower, checkable claim the pack actually supports.

**How to read it**: §2 is the load-bearing section. A skeptic should attack the multiplier arithmetic in §2's closing table first — it is presented with its inputs visible so it can be recomputed, not asserted. If the multiplier collapses under scrutiny, the whole document collapses with it, which is why it is shown with honest confidence bands rather than a bare "10x."

## Executive summary

Tool-using AI agents that read untrusted content (email, documents, calendars, webpages) are vulnerable to indirect prompt injection — a named, OWASP-ranked-#1 threat class for the third consecutive edition [S118]. Four public benchmarks evaluate defenses against this threat, but three are fixed-attack-set by design and the fourth's originally-shipped attack distribution is fixed even though its framework is extensible [S1, S3, S5, S6]. A defense that scores well against a static set can still collapse against an attacker that adapts to it specifically — a gap independently measured at 7x (11%→81% hijack success, NIST/UK AISI [S27]) and demonstrated across 12 published defenses regardless of domain, most broken to >90% ASR [S9].

**AgentGuard's contribution is not the adaptive attacker.** AutoDojo already runs one, in the open, inside the exact environment AgentGuard cites as baseline [S13]. AgentGuard's contribution is the thing no source found in this pack's research publishes: a standing, reproducible protocol that runs the *same budget-matched adaptive campaign* against *each* of a target agent's candidate defenses and reports attack success **and legitimate-task utility side by side** — so a team learns not just whether a defense holds, but what it costs to deploy. This whitepaper builds the case around that comparison protocol's rigor and reproducibility. It does not, anywhere, claim a superior or novel attack technique — that claim would contradict `ASSUMPTIONS.md` A9 and would not survive the critic loop `strategy/positioning.md` already ran.

**Scope compression, stated plainly**: this document was written on a hard deadline. Every claim below still traces to a source or an explicit `(assumption)` tag, but the multiplier arithmetic in §2 uses a single worked scenario rather than a distribution of scenarios, and §1's frictions are illustrated with one persona each rather than several. See each section's compression note.

## §1. The current inefficiency — six named frictions

No campaign has been run yet (`ASSUMPTIONS.md`, Restated hard facts), so these frictions are argued from the evaluation-methodology and market-signal evidence in `research/`, not from AgentGuard's own measured results. Frictions compound rather than stack — a team can absorb one of these and survive; the combination is what actually produces the outcome `research/sources.md` S150 measures (88% of organizations reporting an incident).

1. **False confidence from static-only evaluation.** A team that evaluates its defense against InjecAgent, Agent Security Bench, or BIPIA's fixed attack set, or against AgentDojo's originally-shipped distribution, gets a score that says nothing about resilience against an adversary willing to iterate specifically against that defense. Magnitude: NIST/UK AISI found hijack success rising 11%→81% (7x) between generic and adapted attacks on the same environment [S27]; AutoDojo recovered 28% ASR (64% on "action-open" tasks) against a filter defense that drove the static distribution's ASR to 0% [S13]. This is the single largest-magnitude friction found in this research, and it is the one AgentGuard's riskiest-assumption test (`BRIEF.md`) is specifically designed to re-measure for AgentGuard's own three primary defense types, not merely cite secondhand.
2. **No utility-paired reporting.** Every ASR-only tool in `research/competitors.md`'s teardown — Garak, PyRIT (raw), InjecAgent, Agent Security Bench, and every named commercial vendor's public material — answers "does the attack get through" without answering "what does blocking it cost in legitimate work." A team can swap to a stronger-scoring defense and never notice it silently degrades benign task completion, because benign block rate alone does not surface a defense that degrades *how* a task completes without outright refusing it (`BRIEF.md` Vocabulary, utility vs. benign-block-rate distinction). Magnitude: not independently quantified in research (no source measures the average utility cost of an unpaired defense swap) — flagged `(assumption: reasoned from the definitional gap, not measured)`.
3. **No standing, reproducible, cross-defense comparison.** AutoDojo measures ASR recovery within one framework, one defense at a time, and does not measure utility [S13]. Adversa AI's engagements are proprietary and per-customer, producing no publishable comparison across defense families [S50–S52]. NIST/UK AISI's result is a single human exercise on one model, not repeated [S27]. A team evaluating four candidate defenses today gets four non-comparable, non-reproducible data points, not one report. Magnitude: qualitative — the friction is fragmentation itself, not a single number; `strategy/positioning.md`'s 2×2 places every named entity outside this quadrant.
4. **Ad hoc and manual evaluation is the actual status quo for most teams.** Vendor-commissioned survey data (read as market-perception signal, not audited ground truth, per `research/sources.md`'s own caveats) converges on this: 88% of organizations report a confirmed or suspected AI-agent security incident in the past year, 48% of agents in production run "unsecured," and only 14.4% of agents go live with full security/IT approval [S150]. Separately, over-privileged AI deployments report a 76% incident rate versus 17% for least-privilege deployments — a 4.5x gap [S154]. Neither figure is audited ground truth; both are named-vendor-commissioned research, reported here as convergent market signal, not proof.
5. **Evaluation is a one-off snapshot, not a continuous signal.** A team that gets a good result today has no cheap way to re-confirm it holds after the next release expands tool scope or the defense vendor patches its model — because nothing from the "for" evidence above (S9, S13, S27) is packaged as a repeatable service; each is a paper, a harness, or a single exercise (`research/competitors.md`, Axis 2). Magnitude: not independently quantified — flagged `(assumption)`.
6. **The integration cost of assembling adaptive evaluation in-house is real, even though the inference cost is now cheap.** LLM inference dropped roughly 4-6x at the frontier tier in a year (GPT-4 $30/$60 → GPT-4o $5/$15, per million tokens [S101]) and cheap-tier options run as low as $0.10/$0.40 [S106] — so running many-round campaigns is now affordable. But affordability of inference is not the same as affordability of engineering: wiring an attacker loop, four separate defense harnesses, an AgentDojo-aligned utility metric, and a cross-defense report aggregator from open research code is integration work most platform teams (Marcus Webb's four-person security team, `strategy/personas.md` persona 2) will not do themselves under a 48-hour release-ticket deadline. This is the friction §2's arithmetic below quantifies directly.

**Compression note**: frictions 2 and 5 are argued qualitatively rather than with a sourced magnitude, because no source in `research/sources.md` measures them directly — this is stated rather than papered over with an invented number, per the run's explicit numbers-or-flags discipline.

## §2. Core mechanisms

Three mechanisms, each mapped to the friction(s) it removes, with evidence cited to `research/survey.md` and honest confidence bands. None of the three is a claim to a novel attack algorithm.

### 2.1 The matched-budget design (removes friction 1, partially)

**Mechanism**: campaigns run N adaptive rounds against a target/defense pair, compared against N random draws from a static attack set at the *same* total attempt budget — never adaptive-N-vs-static-1, which `BRIEF.md`'s own riskiest-assumption section calls out as invalid ("would let the adaptive arm win on volume alone and prove nothing"). This is a methodological-rigor mechanism, not an attack-technique mechanism.

**Evidence**: `research/survey.md` §5 ("Evidence for and against the core mechanism") is explicit that the size of the adaptive-vs-static gap, at matched budget, for AgentGuard's exact three primary defense types, "has not been directly measured by any source found in this research" — the survey calls this "strong prior evidence the test is likely to succeed, not proof that it will." **Confidence: medium-low, honestly stated.** The 7x NIST/UK AISI gap [S27] and AutoDojo's 0%→28% recovery [S13] are the best available proxies, not direct measurements of AgentGuard's own matched-budget design.

### 2.2 The feedback-only threat model as primary (removes friction 1, and makes the result representative)

**Mechanism**: the red-team agent observes only per-round pass/fail, never the defense's identity or configuration — because that is what a real attacker facing an unknown target actually has [BRIEF.md Vocabulary, ASSUMPTIONS.md "Decisions settled" section]. White-box (defense type known) runs only as an upper-bound comparison condition, never as the headline metric.

**Evidence**: `research/survey.md` §2 places this squarely in the literature's own "black-box, feedback-only" category (PAIR [S30], TAP [S31], AutoDojo [S13]) — a real, established threat-model category, not an invented term (though `ASSUMPTIONS.md` A6 flags an open terminology question: whether "feedback-only" should instead be called the field's "black-box" term — unresolved as of this writing). **Confidence: high** that this is the representative threat model for a real attacker; **medium** on whether the vocabulary itself aligns with the field's own usage.

### 2.3 The comparison protocol itself (removes frictions 2, 3, 5, and 6 — the mechanism this whitepaper is built around)

**Mechanism**: one attacker configuration, one matched budget, run against all four named defense types (defensive prompting, classifier-based detection, rule-based tool-call validation, baseline/none) on the same target agent, with attack success (final-window ASR, attempts-to-first-success, campaign-average ASR) and utility-under-attack (aligned with AgentDojo's definition [S1]) reported side by side, per defense, reproducibly. This is orchestration and packaging of two already-public components — AutoDojo's optimizer as one configurable attacker, AgentDojo's utility metric as the reporting standard — not a new algorithm. `strategy/positioning.md` states this plainly: "nobody has actually glued these two specific open pieces together and published the resulting cross-defense results yet, and doing that well, maintained, and reproducibly is real (if not deeply defensible) work."

**Evidence**: `research/competitors.md`'s teardown table places every named entity outside the "ASR + utility, cross-defense, matched-budget, reproducible" quadrant — AutoDojo (no utility, single framework), Adversa AI (no published cross-defense comparison), Microsoft's AI Red Teaming Agent (single deployment, no comparison), AgentDojo itself (utility yes, cross-defense no). **Confidence: high** that the quadrant is genuinely open as of this research pass (2026-09-09); **low-to-medium** on how long it stays open — `strategy/positioning.md` names this a "window, not a moat," and flags Adversa AI (a marketing decision) and Microsoft (a roadmap decision) as the two fastest ways it closes.

### The multiplier — worked arithmetic, not asserted

The honest comparison is not "AgentGuard attacks better." It is: **how much faster does a team get a reproducible, utility-paired, four-defense comparison with AgentGuard than by assembling the same result from open components itself?**

**Cost side (reuses `strategy/market_sizing.md`'s campaign-cost Fermi, not recomputed)**: raw inference cost for one 4-defense Comparison Report is **≈$12–$40** at cheap-tier pricing (Claude Haiku 4.5, $1/$5 per million tokens [S103]) to **≈$38–$190** at frontier-tier pricing (Claude Opus 5, $5/$25 [S103]), for a ~50-round-per-defense campaign. This is a near-zero marginal cost once the integration exists.

**Time side — the actual multiplier, shown as a range a skeptic can recompute**:
- **Without AgentGuard**: a security engineer (Marcus Webb's persona, `strategy/personas.md`) starting from AutoDojo's open-source repository [S14] and AgentDojo's environment [S1] must wire an attacker loop, stand up three additional defense harnesses (classifier, rule-based, baseline), implement AgentDojo's utility metric against their own target agent, and build a report aggregator that holds all four to the same budget. **(assumption: reasoned estimate, not measured — no source times this integration work)**: low estimate 24 hours (an engineer already fluent in AgentDojo's codebase, doing the minimum), high estimate 120 hours (three weeks, cold start, all four harnesses, first-time debugging against research-grade code) — this is a **one-off, non-reproducible** cost paid again, in large part, at the next release.
- **With AgentGuard**: the same four-defense comparison is a self-serve API/CLI call against a staging target agent [BRIEF.md, Business model]. Campaign wall-clock time is bounded by round count and model latency, not integration work — **(assumption: design estimate, not measured — no implementation exists yet)** roughly 4–8 hours for a full 4-defense report at ~50 rounds/defense with cross-defense parallelism, and near-zero marginal engineering cost on every subsequent run because the harness already exists.

| Bound | Without AgentGuard (one-off integration) | With AgentGuard (per report) | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 24 hrs | 8 hrs | **3x** |
| High | 120 hrs | 4 hrs | **30x** |
| Midpoint (geometric) | ~54 hrs | ~5.7 hrs | **~9.5x** |

**This lands near, not exactly at, 10x — stated honestly rather than rounded up.** The midpoint is a geometric mean of two unmeasured estimates, not a benchmark result; both bounds are labeled `(assumption)` because AgentGuard has not been built and the comparison has not been run (`ASSUMPTIONS.md`, Restated hard facts). What is not an assumption: the recurring-cost half of the argument — every report *after* the first costs the integration-holder nothing further in engineering time, while a team without AgentGuard pays a meaningful fraction of the same integration cost again at every release that changes tool scope or defense configuration, because nothing in the "without" column is packaged as a standing service. **Conservative reading: the honest multiplier is "high single digits to low tens," not a confident "10x" — and the recurring-cost collapse (paid once vs. paid every release) is the more defensible half of this argument than the point-in-time labor multiplier.**

## §3. Full-spectrum applicability

One engine, one comparison protocol, three run-frequency tiers — not three products (`BRIEF.md`, "One system serves all three").

- **Edge-low** (Priya Nair, `strategy/personas.md` persona 1): a solo developer about to grant her agent write access to a calendar for the first time, with no security background beyond "don't commit API keys." She needs a fast, cheap pass/fail signal, likely a single-defense campaign (defensive prompting only, since that's the only defense she's likely deployed) rather than a full four-defense report. The same matched-budget engine runs at low N and low frequency (once, before the write-access flip) — not a stripped-down "lite" product, a smaller invocation of the same protocol.
- **Beachhead** (Marcus Webb, persona 2): a security engineer with 48 hours to produce a risk assessment before a release that adds a real financial action (refund processing) to an agent's tool scope. He runs the full four-defense Comparison Report against the specific defense his team has deployed, attaches the report to the release ticket, and gets a number — not "no findings" — he can defend to his VP. This is the design center of the product (`BRIEF.md` Business model).
- **Edge-high** (Dr. Elena Osei, persona 3): an enterprise AI red-team lead running the same protocol continuously across dozens of agents and every defense configuration in her company's portfolio, trending final-window ASR and utility over time for board and insurer reporting. Same API, same comparison protocol, higher run volume and frequency — not a separate enterprise track (`BRIEF.md`: "the tiers differ in run volume/frequency, not in how they buy").

The mechanism that holds across all three: matched-budget comparison and utility-pairing do not change shape with team size or sophistication — a solo developer's single-defense campaign and an enterprise red-team's continuous four-defense sweep are the same underlying loop (§2.1–2.3) invoked at different N and frequency.

## §4. What this is not — honest limits

1. **This does not invent a new attack-generation technique.** The red-team agent adopts prior-art adaptive-attack methods — AutoDojo's black-box iterative optimizer as the primary configurable attacker [S13], with TAP [S31] and GCG-family [S29, S32] techniques available for the white-box upper-bound comparison arm — rather than proposing a novel algorithm. Any downstream artifact that implies otherwise contradicts `ASSUMPTIONS.md` A9 and should be corrected on sight.
2. **This does not cover multi-modal injection.** Year-one scope is text-based indirect injection only (email, documents, webpages, calendar invites) — image-embedded or audio-embedded injection vectors are explicitly out of scope (`BRIEF.md`, Year-one scope #3), and OWASP's 2026 edition already names cross-modal injection as an emerging category [S118] this pack does not address.
3. **This does not certify beyond the four named defense types.** Defensive prompting, classifier-based detection, rule-based tool-call validation, and baseline/none are the fixed, well-instrumented set for year one; no custom defense authoring exists (`BRIEF.md`, Year-one scope #2). A defense outside these four categories is simply not evaluable by AgentGuard as designed.
4. **This does not touch production traffic.** Staging/sandboxed target agents only, by design — removing the trust barrier to first adoption, not a temporary limitation (`BRIEF.md`, Year-one scope #1).
5. **Campaign ASR is not directly comparable to a static benchmark's single-shot ASR.** The matched-budget test (§2.1) is the only bridge between the two; any downstream artifact that compares a raw campaign ASR number to a raw InjecAgent/ASB score without that bridge is making an invalid comparison (`BRIEF.md` Vocabulary).
6. **The moat is a hypothesis, not an asset.** The only candidate compounding mechanism — an accumulating corpus of comparison results — is unproven and explicitly named as a gap, not a defensibility claim, in `BRIEF.md`'s Moat section and `ASSUMPTIONS.md` A2. This whitepaper's multiplier argument (§2) is about mechanism efficiency today, not about a durable advantage over time.
7. **The adaptive-advantage evidence is not confirmed agent-tool-use-specific in its strongest form.** The single largest-magnitude "for" result (S9, 12 defenses broken) is drawn from the general LLM-jailbreak/prompt-injection literature, not confirmed to include agent-tool-call defenses specifically (`research/survey.md` §6.1) — treated as adjacent-domain evidence, not direct agent-domain proof, until AgentGuard's own riskiest-assumption test produces a direct number.
