We are four graduate students finishing CMPE 295A/295B at San José State University, and this document is deliberately honest about what that does and doesn't give us.

It doesn't give us a founder story in the usual sense. None of us has run a security company before. None of us has sold enterprise software, built a go-to-market motion, or shipped a product that a paying customer has ever used. We have no prior company, no domain-operating history in AI security, and no personal origin story about a breach that changed our lives — if we wrote one, it would not be true, and this document exists specifically so that nobody has to take our word for embellishments we didn't make.

What we do have is two semesters of direct, sustained research engagement with the exact literature this product is built on top of. We read AgentDojo, InjecAgent, Agent Security Bench, and BIPIA closely enough to know which ones are genuinely static by design and which one — AgentDojo — is an extensible environment whose *originally-shipped attack distribution* is what's fixed, not the framework itself. We didn't take that distinction from a marketing summary; we found it by reading the paper. We traced AutoDojo's black-box optimizer closely enough to adopt its technique deliberately, as one configurable attacker, rather than either reinventing it or pretending it doesn't already exist — and closely enough to recognize that competing with it on attack sophistication, against a team with a real research budget, would be a fight we'd lose on the merits. We found the joint OpenAI/Anthropic/DeepMind paper showing 12 of 12 published defenses broken under adaptive pressure, and let it correct an assumption in our own original brief rather than defending a claim the evidence didn't support.

That correction is the part of this story we're most willing to stand behind. Our original pitch was "we automate the adaptive adversary; existing benchmarks are static." Research surfaced that this was only half true — AutoDojo already automates adaptive attacks, a human NIST/UK AI Security Institute exercise already demonstrated the adaptive-vs-static gap at scale, and a funded commercial vendor, Adversa AI, already markets a mechanism close to our original framing. We didn't quietly keep the old pitch because it sounded better. We logged the correction, rejected three replacement differentiators that didn't survive scrutiny, and landed on a narrower, more checkable claim: the comparison across defenses is missing, not the attacker. That's a smaller claim than we started with, and it's the one we can actually defend.

The edge this gives us is research access and rigor, not distribution or go-to-market experience — and we're not claiming otherwise. We can read the literature this space is built on as closely as anyone doing it as a day job, because for two semesters, reading it closely *is* the job. Whether that edge is enough to build something that outlasts a graduate program is an open question this document doesn't resolve. The riskiest-assumption test — a two-week, matched-budget comparison that could return a null result and end the thesis honestly — is the next thing that has to happen before we'd claim anything more than what's written here.
