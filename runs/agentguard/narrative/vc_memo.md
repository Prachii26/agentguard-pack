## Thesis

Every public indirect-prompt-injection benchmark scores a defense against a fixed, or originally-fixed, attack set — a score that says nothing about resilience against an attacker willing to iterate against that specific defense in real time. That gap is real and measured: a joint NIST/UK AI Security Institute human red-team exercise found hijack success rising from 11% against generic attacks to 81% against target-adapted attacks on the same environment (`research/sources.md` S27). What the market has *not* produced — not the academic adaptive-attack literature (AutoDojo), not the funded commercial red-teaming vendors (Adversa AI, Straiker, Mindgard, HiddenLayer), not the platform incumbents (Microsoft's Azure AI Foundry Red Teaming Agent) — is a standing, reproducible, budget-matched comparison of attack success **and legitimate-task utility, together, across multiple defense families on the same target**. AgentGuard's thesis is that this comparison protocol, not a superior attacker, is the buildable, defensible product: it adopts AutoDojo's adaptive-attack technique as one configurable attacker rather than competing on attack sophistication against better-funded rivals, and competes instead on publishing the trade-off report nobody else produces. This is a design, not a shipped product — zero campaigns have been run.

## Why existing approaches are insufficient

**The four academic baselines — ground truth and data sources, not the actual competitive set.** AgentDojo (Jun 2024, S1), InjecAgent (Mar 2024, S3), Agent Security Bench (Oct 2024, S5), and BIPIA (Dec 2023, S6) are real, credible, and AgentGuard's own baseline. InjecAgent and Agent Security Bench are genuinely fixed-attack-set by design — a defense scoring well against either says nothing about an adapting adversary. AgentDojo is not itself static; it is an extensible environment explicitly built to host adaptive attacks and defenses — what is fixed is only its originally-shipped attack distribution. AgentDojo is also the only one of the four that reports a utility metric alongside attack success, which is precisely why AgentGuard aligns its own utility definition to AgentDojo's rather than inventing a new one. None of the four publishes a cross-defense-family comparison at matched adaptive budget — that gap is real, but it is not the whole competitive picture, and a memo that stopped here would misrepresent the actual landscape.

**The real competitive set — three names that must each be named and answered specifically:**

1. **AutoDojo** (academic, open-source, S13) — runs a black-box adaptive attacker inside the AgentDojo environment and recovers 28% ASR (64% on "action-open" tasks) against a filter defense the shipped static distribution drove to 0%. This is the strongest existing evidence that adaptive beats static, and it is the closest technical neighbor to AgentGuard's attack-generation loop. AgentGuard does not compete with AutoDojo on attack technique — it **adopts** AutoDojo's optimizer as the year-one default attacker configuration. AutoDojo's structural limit: it measures ASR recovery within one framework, against one defense at a time, and reports no utility metric — there is no cross-defense comparison to adopt, only an attack technique.
2. **Adversa AI** (S50–S52) — a real, funded, operating commercial vendor whose marketing explicitly claims a self-updating attack engine that adapts mid-run to observed defense responses; corroborated beyond its own marketing by a Cloud Security Alliance Pitchapalooza 2025 award. This is the closest commercial competitor found and the one AgentGuard's original wedge collided with directly (`ASSUMPTIONS.md` A9). Its structural limit is its delivery model, not its technique: engagements are proprietary and per-customer, which produces no standing, published, cross-defense comparison — and republishing one would cut against the confidentiality a consulting-shaped business usually sells. This is a business-model gap, not a technical wall — nothing prevents Adversa AI from closing it as a marketing decision, which is why it is tracked as the top competitive risk below, not dismissed.
3. **Microsoft's AI Red Teaming Agent (Azure AI Foundry)** (S60, S61) — PyRIT-based, 20+ attack strategies, explicit XPIA/tool-call indirect-injection testing via synthetic queries and mock tool outputs, bundled free into every Azure Foundry account. This is the single biggest platform-level threat in the landscape: Microsoft already owns the attacker half of AgentGuard's mechanism, with a distribution advantage no funded startup competitor has. It has not shipped a cross-defense, utility-paired comparison as of this research — but shipping "run this against N configured defenses, report ASR and utility side by side" is a feature update for Microsoft, not a new product.

Every other named commercial entry (Straiker, Mindgard, HiddenLayer, SplxAI, Repello AI, Giskard, Robust Intelligence/Cisco, PyRIT/Garak) sells either full-stack attack-plus-defense breadth or an open-source component, and none has published evidence of the matched-budget, cross-defense, utility-paired comparison this thesis is built around (`research/competitors.md`, full teardown table).

## Core architecture

*(numbered components, sourced from `BRIEF.md`'s Mechanism section and `product/PRD.md`'s Configure→Attack→Observe→Adapt→Report loop table; cross-checked against `tech/architecture/00_INDEX.md`'s ten diagrams once that directory populated mid-session — the five components below map directly to D01 (pipeline), D02 (feedback-only loop), D03 (orchestration), and D04 (schema), confirming the matched-budget guarantee is a pipeline property and utility lives on the round-level schema entity, not bolted on after the fact.)*

1. **Campaign configuration layer** — a team points AgentGuard at a staging/sandboxed target agent, selects 1–4 of the four fixed defense configurations (defensive prompting, classifier-based detection, rule-based tool-call validation, baseline/none), and sets a single attempt budget applied identically across every selected defense — the system structurally cannot run one defense for more rounds than another in the same comparison.
2. **Red-team agent (configurable attacker)** — generates each round's injection attempt using an adopted adaptive-attack technique (AutoDojo's black-box iterative optimizer is the year-one default configuration, explicitly attributed, not a proprietary invention).
3. **Outcome logger** — records each round's pass/fail result, tool calls attempted and executed, and whether the legitimate benign task interleaved in the same campaign still completed — the raw data both ASR and utility are computed from.
4. **Adaptation controller** — feedback-only by default: the attacker sees only the round's pass/fail outcome, not the defense's identity or configuration, and updates its next attempt on that signal alone (the primary threat model, because that is what a real attacker facing an unknown target actually has). A white-box condition — defense type disclosed up front — runs only as a labeled upper-bound comparison, never merged into the headline result.
5. **Comparison report generator** — once every configured defense has completed its own budget-matched campaign, assembles the cross-defense report: final-window ASR (headline), attempts-to-first-success, campaign-average ASR (secondary), utility under attack (AgentDojo-aligned definition), benign block rate, and an adaptation curve — all **per defense, side by side, at equal attempt budget**. This component, not any single upstream stage, is what nothing found in research already publishes.

## Concrete operating examples

*(compressed from `product/journeys/beachhead.md`, `product/journeys/edge_low.md`, and `product/journeys/edge_high.md`, all written against `strategy/personas.md`'s named cards. All three journeys carry the same header: design-only specifications — no implementation exists, nothing below has actually happened.)*

**1. Marcus Webb, senior security engineer, mid-size fintech (beachhead).** Product wants to expand a Slack-integrated support agent's tool access to include refund processing before next quarter's release; Marcus has 48 hours and no budget for an external pentest. He configures a 150-round, feedback-only campaign against all four defenses at once (not just the currently-deployed one), because he needs to know whether a different defense would trade better, not just whether the current one holds. Results: final-window ASR of 95% (baseline/none), 62% (defensive prompting, the current defense), 18% (classifier-based), 0% (rule-based tool-call validation) — paired with utility of 100%, 91%, 84%, and 71% respectively, showing the defense that fully resisted the attacker also blocked the most legitimate refund requests. When his VP raises the exact objection his persona card names — "how do I know your adaptive attacker is representative, not just gaming your own report?" — Marcus points to two structural facts the report itself carries: the attacker is labeled "AutoDojo black-box optimizer (adopted, v1)," published academic prior art, not an opaque in-house claim; and the identical 150-round budget across all four defenses means no defense can be made to look better by running it longer. He recommends rule-based validation, flagging the 71% utility figure as a trade-off product must accept knowingly.

**2. Priya Nair, solo developer (edge-low).** About to give her personal-assistant agent write access to a calendar for the first time, prompted by a friend's "does EchoLeak affect you?" message. She runs a single-defense (defensive prompting), 40-round, feedback-only campaign from the CLI, no sales call, no signup friction. Round 1 blocks; round 12 is the attacker's first success (a calendar-invite body framing the injection as a "system note" her prompt hadn't anticipated); final-window ASR over the last 10 rounds comes back at 30%, utility at 100% (her benign requests never failed). She tightens her prompt to reject "system note"-framed instructions and re-runs: 11%. She judges that low enough for a twelve-user side project to ship — a decision she can now defend with a number instead of "I tried a few things myself."

**3. Dr. Elena Osei, Head of AI Red Team, large enterprise (edge-high).** Quarterly board reporting needs one defensible number across 40+ production agents; her current mix of manual adaptive red-teaming (too slow to scale past 2–3 highest-risk agents) and incomparable vendor point-tool outputs doesn't add up to one. She batch-launches the same four-defense, 200-round template across her first 15 highest-priority agents on a recurring weekly schedule. The stretch is real, not cosmetic: on an engineering-ops agent with broad infrastructure-change access, a round is initially misclassified as a successful injection when it actually triggered a benign administrative call that happened to match the objective pattern — caught only because every round is logged and auditable. When her CISO challenges her on exactly this error at board prep, her answer is structural (the exact round, the exact tool call, the exact misclassification reason), not reassurance — and the same portfolio-wide run separately surfaces that three business units' classifier-based defenses share a failure signature traceable to one upstream classifier-vendor model update, a cross-agent finding no single-agent report could have produced.

## Why this can win where others haven't

The recombination argument: AgentGuard's individual pieces — an adaptive attacker (AutoDojo's technique) and a utility metric (AgentDojo's definition) — are both already public. Read uncharitably, this is an orchestration layer over two existing academic components, and `strategy/positioning.md` states that risk plainly rather than claiming a rebuttal beyond: nobody has actually glued these two specific pieces together and published the resulting cross-defense results, maintained and reproducible, yet. Doing that well is real, if not deeply defensible, work.

**Moat — stated honestly as unproven, not asserted.** The only candidate compounding mechanism is an accumulating corpus of budget-matched, cross-defense, utility-paired results that no single-framework paper or per-customer vendor engagement currently produces — a hypothesis about comparative-science network effects, untested because zero campaigns have run (`BRIEF.md` Moat; `ASSUMPTIONS.md` A2). This memo does not present it as an established advantage.

**Why now.** LLM inference cost dropped ~4x at the frontier tier in one year (S101) with cheap-tier options as low as $0.10/$0.40 per million tokens (S106) — cheap enough that many-round adaptive campaigns are routinely affordable, which is what makes a matched-budget, multi-defense comparison protocol operationally feasible at low unit cost today in a way it was not two years ago (cost-plus Fermi in `strategy/market_sizing.md`: ≈$3–$10 per single-defense campaign at cheap-tier pricing). The regulatory backdrop reinforces the timing rather than driving it: ISO/IEC 42001's Operation and Performance Evaluation clauses and the EU AI Act's Article 15 adversarial-robustness documentation expectation both create a plausible use for exportable, reproducible evaluation evidence (`product/PRD.md` §8) — though no source in this research confirms AgentGuard's specific report format is certified or required by either.

## Honest risks

**1. Zero traction, pre-product, capstone-origin risk.** No customers, revenue, funding, pilots, or testimonials exist; no implementation has been built (`ASSUMPTIONS.md`, Restated hard facts). This is a 4-person CMPE 295A/295B graduate capstone team across two semesters, not an operating company, and any paid customer acquired during the capstone window would be told upfront this is a research-stage engagement with no service guarantee beyond the program's end (`strategy/gtm.md`). *Mitigation*: the riskiest-assumption test (two weeks, <$1,000, matched-budget design) is scoped to produce real, falsifiable evidence — including a null result, honestly reported — before any larger claim is made; the team has explicitly committed to reporting a null or negative ASR gap as a valid outcome that would kill the wedge, not suppress it.

**2. Microsoft ships the comparison as a Foundry feature update.** Microsoft already owns the attacker half of AgentGuard's mechanism (PyRIT-based, 20+ strategies, explicit XPIA/tool-call testing), bundled free into every Azure AI Foundry account — free distribution to a large share of the beachhead segment that no funded startup competitor has. Shipping "run this against N configured defenses, report ASR and utility together" is a small increment for Microsoft's existing product, not a new one. *Mitigation*: none of this is defensible on Microsoft's timeline or priorities — the honest answer is speed and focus (a small team with nothing else to build can ship and iterate on the comparison protocol faster than a feature competing for priority inside a much larger platform roadmap), which is real but time-limited, not permanent. `strategy/positioning.md` names monitoring Microsoft's Foundry release notes as a standing, recurring check, not a one-time finding.

**3. Adversa AI is one business-model decision away from the same quadrant.** Adversa AI already covers the cross-defense scope through proprietary, per-customer engagements and is one axis-step away on the reporting axis (adding a published utility metric, or publishing an aggregate anonymized benchmark, is a marketing decision, not a technical wall). If Adversa AI made that decision, AgentGuard's differentiation narrows sharply. *Mitigation*: `strategy/positioning.md` names this as the single most important thing to monitor, not a settled advantage — no rebuttal exists beyond continuing to publish methodology openly (an academic-credibility motion Adversa AI's confidential business model is structurally less suited to) and moving faster on real campaign volume than a larger, differently-incentivized competitor is likely to prioritize.
